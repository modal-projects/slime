🎯 Recommended research question

The question

Does restarting a quarter of each GRPO batch from recent snapshotted states buy more learning per GPU-hour than generating every episode from scratch?

Stated as a wager: recovery and continued optimization are the skills the fresh-start distribution rarely exercises, because an episode that has already gone wrong is also an episode that has already spent its budget. Retro replay pays to revisit those states. The claim is only meaningful if the comparison holds compute fixed.

🔬 Assessment

Why the idea is promising

Eight continuations from the exact same state form a cleaner comparison than eight independent episodes. They condition out prefix variance, reuse expensive tool work, and directly expose whether the policy can improve or repair a concrete partial solution. Frontier-CS is suitable because it has continuous scores, iterative submissions, and observable regressions.

What must be claimed carefully

A past snapshot changes the reset-state distribution. This is not an unbiased policy gradient for episodes starting from the original problem. It is a mixed reset curriculum: suffix actions are generated by a recent rollout actor while states are sampled from a replay distribution up to four updates old. The fresh pool is configured with a one-batch prefetch cap, but observed fresh behavior-version lag reached three updates because long episodes crossed weight-sync boundaries. Start-state evaluation must remain the scientific endpoint.

What PrefixRL changes in the interpretation
PrefixRL reports 2× compute efficiency over its strongest SFT+RL baseline, more than 3× relative final training accuracy, and transfer from prefixed training to unprefixed evaluation (“back-generalization”). Its theory assumes correct, realizable prefixes. Our promising/recovery snapshots are often partial or regressed rather than prefixes of successful traces, so those guarantees do not transfer directly. The paper strengthens the case for a shared unprefixed/start-state evaluation and for treating branch position as a difficulty-control knob.
What the experience-replay paper changes in the interpretation
The replay-buffer paper reports up to 40% lower compute while preserving or improving accuracy, and frames buffer design as a three-way trade-off among inference cost, staleness, and sample diversity. Its replay ratio is the number of trainer uses per generated trajectory. Current retro does not obtain that saving: each snapshot is one-use and every restored sibling still requires fresh suffix inference. Retro currently amortizes prefix/tool work and changes the reset-state distribution; it does not reuse completed policy-gradient samples.

🔁 Worker control plane and replay-buffer comparison
Trainer workers

Megatron trainers consume one complete mixed batch, run the optimizer step, and own the authoritative weights. The async driver starts generation of batch t+1 before training batch t.

Inference workers

SGLang engines continuously answer episode-generation requests using their current rollout-weight copy. With update_weights_interval=1, the trainer waits for generation t+1 to finish, pauses inference, pushes the newly trained weights, flushes caches, and resumes generation.

What is queued

Fresh candidate groups enter the persistent fully-async worker’s active-task/output queue. Passing groups are removed and trained once. Retro’s persistent store contains snapshot manifests and executable states—not completed rollout groups—and each leased snapshot is consumed once.

🧩 Minimal algorithm

Training mixture

Keep 32 groups fixed: 24 fresh and 8 retro. Each retro group restores one snapshot into eight independent sandboxes. Siblings share group_index but retain distinct sample and rollout IDs.

New suffix, masked replay prefix

Reconstruct conditioning exactly, loss-mask the inherited prefix, and record logprobs only for new suffix tokens. Pin retro siblings to one behavior version; hard-gate behavior lag separately from the four-update snapshot-state age and monitor importance-ratio ESS.

One simple reward

Train on final fractional verifier score. Log improvement over snapshot and branch-local best, but do not shape on them initially or inherit the parent’s best submission.

Same remaining horizon

Give each sibling only the parent’s remaining step and wall-time budget. A branch cannot receive 75 fresh turns from a 70%-complete state without changing compute and horizon.

Final reward is the right first version
Within one sibling group, subtracting the snapshot score from every final reward is an additive constant. GRPO centering removes it, so absolute final reward and improvement reward produce the same normalized advantages. Train on final reward; log improvement for analysis.

Formal objective: optimize 0.75 × start-state return + 0.25 × return from the selected recent-state distribution. This is a deliberate reset curriculum, not an unchanged start-state objective.

🌿 Which trajectories and turns should branch?
Choose branchable states, not merely good or bad trajectories
Good or solved states often make every sibling succeed; arbitrary bad states often make every sibling fail. Both yield zero-variance groups. The useful target is a state whose current-policy continuations are likely to diverge—especially a repairable state or an unsolved state with demonstrated progress and budget left.

Advance beyond the pilot only if retro replay improves full held-out Frontier-CS reward AUC or solve rate per GPU-hour, while increasing informative-group yield and measured recovery, with no material degradation in asynchronous staleness, submission load, stability, or USACO transfer.

⏱️ Actual asynchronous and staleness semantics
Training loop

async_mode=True and update_weights_interval=1. Rollout t+1 is generated while rollout t trains, then the trainer waits for generation to finish before pushing the next actor weights.

24 fresh groups

The mixed-rollout function capped the fresh worker's in-flight pool at 24 groups (one batch of prefetch). That is a capacity cap, not a staleness bound: no per-group version check existed anywhere in the selection path.

Measured on the P50-85 lineage (63 steps): fresh behavior-version lag reached 3 updates, up to 4 behavior versions appeared in one batch, every step contained an episode straddling 2 versions (mid-episode weight sync), and 90% of steps had nonzero fresh lag. “Effectively one-update-stale” was too strong.

8 retro groups

The restored state/prefix may come from policy age 0–4. Continuations measured lag 0 and a single behavior version on every step — single-version is enforced by an explicit crossed-version drop, but lag 0 was an accident of the sequential fresh-then-retro schedule, not a guarantee.

“Max age 4” describes replay-state provenance, not four generations of queued fresh rollouts.

Concise interpretation for the completed cohort
The 24 fresh groups are mostly-fresh async data whose tail reached three updates of behavior lag; the 8 retro groups combine a 0–4-update-old reset state with a measured lag-0, single-version continuation policy. Neither lane had an enforced behavior-lag bound; only the replay state carried the four-update age window by construction.
Staleness semantics going forward (post-2026-08-18 code)
The single rollout_max_staleness knob is split into orthogonal, enforced axes: rollout_prefetch_batches sizes the in-flight pool (throughput only); rollout_max_behavior_lag is a hard per-group bound checked at batch assembly on both lanes — a group whose oldest token was generated more than K updates before the trainer's publishing version (rollout t generates under absolute weight version t+1) is discarded and its prompt requeued; snapshot-state age stays a separate axis (RETRO_MIN/MAX_POLICY_AGE). New metrics async/*/behavior_lag report true staleness vs the trainer (the old version_lag is relative to the freshest in-batch sibling and reads 0 on a uniformly stale batch). The fresh and retro legs now generate concurrently on the shared engine fleet, removing the retro leg's added wall time; one consequence is that same-step (age-0) snapshots are no longer leasable, so effective snapshot age becomes 1–4. Mixed-version episodes remain admitted by design — the bound is on the oldest token, not on within-episode uniformity.
What the completed curves currently support
P25 and P50 are effectively tied on terminal reward, while P75 is 0.046 below P50 but uses 45% fewer submissions. Across the full 85 updates, P50 has the highest mean final reward (0.307), followed by P25 (0.299) and P75 (0.293). This supports a quality–continuation-length trade-off, not a definitive best branch position.

Why the new arms trail old P50 on fractional reward — horizon compression, not staleness
Prefetch 4 loaded the engines to ~22–48 concurrent streams per engine (vs 1–4 before), which cut per-stream decode to ~52–54 tok/s and pushed budget_hit_frac to 0.13 (rlag4) / 0.23 (rlag8) against ~0.01 for old P50. Roughly one episode in five to one in four now dies on the 1800 s wall mid-solution, so the policy trains on truncated horizons. Notably solved_frac moved the other way — 0.0315 / 0.0234 vs old P50's 0.0185, whose solve rate declined from 0.029 to 0.019 over training — so the fractional-reward gap looks like partial-credit loss from cut episodes rather than worse policy quality. The two arms also differ from each other on this axis (rlag8 slower and more budget-limited), which is a nuisance imbalance, not a treatment effect.
Instrumentation caveat: the lag-rejection spin loop
Both runs shipped with a bug (fixed in-tree after launch, not in these images): lag-rejected groups were requeued without resetting their sample state, and generate_and_rm short-circuits COMPLETED samples, so a once-stale group recycled buffer → worker → reject indefinitely. This inflates behavior_lag/rejected_groups and dynamic_sampling/completed_groups to the thousands per step in later steps — those two counters must be ignored for these runs. Training correctness is unaffected (rejected groups never entered a batch), but the effective prompt pool shrank as groups were poisoned, which is an additional uncontrolled difference from old P50.
What this does and does not settle
Settled: the per-lane gate works as specified (fresh lag pinned at its ceiling of 4; retro lag measured at ~0.46), and retro-lane bounds above 1 are inert on the current on-demand schedule. Not settled: whether genuinely stale retro continuations help or hurt — that needs cross-step buffering of pre-generated continuation groups so realized retro lag can actually reach 4 or 8. Also unsettled: the concurrent-lane stack's reward standing versus old P50, because prefetch 4 confounds it with horizon compression. The clean follow-up is prefetch 2 (≈24 streams/engine) with an 1800 s budget, or prefetch 4 with a raised episode budget.

What the retro bound can and cannot do — read before interpreting
The lag bound is an admission gate, not a lag generator. Retro continuations are generated on demand inside the collection window of the step that trains them, and at most one weight push lands per window, so realized retro continuation lag is structurally ≤1 regardless of the bound. Bounds 4 and 8 are therefore provably non-binding: the only mechanical difference vs the legacy lane is that mixed-version continuations (span 2, previously dropped and regenerated) are now admitted. rlag4 vs rlag8 is expected to be a null comparison — same admission behavior, differing only by sandbox-timing nondeterminism — and functions as a seed-replicate / sanity arm. A real lag-4/8 retro treatment would require buffering pre-generated continuation groups across steps (a retro analogue of the fresh prefetch queue), which does not exist yet.
